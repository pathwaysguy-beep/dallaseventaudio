Fog effects for your wedding grand entrance and your first dance.

Two moments at a wedding reception get remembered by everyone in the room: the doors opening, and the first song. A low-lying fog effect changes both, but an entrance and a first dance are two different cues, two different densities and, usually, two different machines. Here is how the effect works, what your venue will ask about, and how it gets run so it lands on the beat instead of near it.

Why couples ask for it

Fog is the cheapest way to change a room that isn’t about the room at all.

Most of what a couple can do to a reception space works on the walls: linens, florals, uplighting, drape. A low fog effect works on the floor, which is the one surface every guest is already looking at during the two moments that matter.

A cloud of fog sitting at ankle height does three things at once. It separates the two of you from everything behind you, so the eye goes where you are instead of to the buffet line. It gives the light something to land on, which is why a fog-lit dance floor photographs like a film still and a dry one photographs like a floor. And it moves, slowly, but it moves, so a static shot of two people standing still has motion in it anyway.

It also travels well across styles. A wedding going for a fairytale look and a wedding going for something sleek and modern can use the same effect and get completely different pictures out of it, because what the cloud looks like is decided by what is lighting it.

Types of wedding fog

Four things get called “fog,” and only one of them makes a cloud you can stand on.

This is a common source of confusion when couples start comparing what different vendors are describing, because the same word covers effects that behave nothing like each other. Worth knowing which one you are asking for.

The clouds look

Low-lying dry ice fog

A dense, ground-hugging cloud that sits at ankle height and holds its shape for the length of a song. This is the effect people mean when they say “dancing on the clouds”. It stays down, it does not drift into the rest of the room, and it keeps your faces clear above it for the photographer.

Best for First dance, parent dances, a walk-out entrance across an open floor.
Fills the space

Atmospheric fog

Thicker output that disperses upward and fills a volume rather than hugging the floor. It reads dramatically in a doorway or across an outdoor aisle, and it thins out on its own fairly quickly, which is what you want for a short burst rather than a sustained look.

Best for Grand entrances, a reveal moment, outdoor ceremony exits.
You won’t see it

Haze

A very fine particle that spreads evenly through the air and is essentially invisible on its own. Haze does not give you a cloud. It gives you a room where light beams become visible, so every moving light and every color in the rig suddenly has a shaft attached to it.

Best for The dancing portion of the night, when the lighting rig is doing the work.
Colored by light

“Colored” fog

Nothing tints the fog itself. The cloud is white and the color comes from what is pointed at it, which is good news, because it means the same cloud can be blush for your first dance and deep blue an hour later. Matching your palette is an uplighting decision rather than a fog one.

Best for Anyone who has picked wedding colors and wants the floor to match them.

If you only want one of these, it is almost always the first one.

The low-lying dry ice effect is what the Dancing on the Clouds setup produces, and it is the wedding effect couples ask us for most often. The other three tend to get added around it once a couple has seen what the floor looks like.

Fog machine wedding entrance

An entrance and a first dance want opposite things from the same machine.

For an entrance, the fog is doing crowd work. It fills the doorway and the floor in front of it so that when the doors open you are walking out of a cloud rather than into an empty room. The cloud gets laid down in roughly the thirty seconds before the doors move, heavy enough to read on camera and thin enough that the guests craning to see you can still see you. Because it is a moving shot, the effect is run as a short burst and then allowed to thin as you cross the floor.

For a first dance, the fog is doing the opposite job. It sits still, it stays low, and it has to hold for three or four minutes so the two of you appear to be standing on a surface rather than in a haze. That is a different setup, a different density and, on most floors, a different machine from a doorway burst.

Plenty of couples want both, and both fit on the same setup night with the same technician, two cues instead of one. What matters is saying so early, so the room is prepared for each. On our events that goes into the timeline with your DJ or coordinator before anyone touches a machine.

How the effect works

Why dry ice fog stays on the floor.

The whole “clouds” look comes down to one thing: temperature. The cloud stays on the floor because it leaves the machine colder and heavier than the air in the room.

Dry ice is frozen carbon dioxide, and it sits at roughly a hundred and nine degrees below zero Fahrenheit. It never becomes a liquid. It goes straight from solid to gas. Lowered into a tank of heated water it sublimates fast, and the rush of carbon dioxide drags a load of condensed water vapor out with it. That mixture leaves the machine far colder and noticeably denser than the air in your reception room, so it does what any dense cold thing does: it sinks, then spreads sideways across the floor.

It disappears for the same reason it stays down. As the cloud warms toward room temperature it loses density, thins, and lifts. That is why the effect is cued to a specific song rather than left running, and it is why there is nothing to clean up afterwards.

A ballroom at 78 degrees thins the cloud noticeably faster than the same ballroom at 68.

Which is why the venue, the season and the time of day get asked about before the effect is quoted, an outdoor floor on a July evening in Texas is a genuinely different problem from the same floor indoors in November.
Planning it in

Four things worth settling early.

Each of these is easy to settle in advance, and each one is annoying if it surfaces on the day instead of three months before it.

Your venueAsk first, not last

Raise the effect with your venue coordinator as soon as it is on your list. Some venues have protocols around fog, and a handful of older buildings have detection systems that want a conversation in advance. Every one of these is straightforward to work around when it is known early and awkward when it turns up at load-in. We are used to having that conversation directly with venue staff if it is easier.

The floor itselfWhere the cloud will go

Low fog spreads across whatever surface it lands on, so a dance floor flush with the rest of the room behaves differently from a raised platform with a step down at the edge. Neither is a problem. They are just set up differently, and knowing which one you have decides where the machine sits.

TimingCued to the music

The difference between a good fog effect and a forgettable one is timing. The cloud should be at full depth the moment you step onto the floor, not building while you stand there waiting for it. That means the technician running the effect is cueing against the music with your DJ or band, which is the reason we keep someone on it all night rather than starting it and walking away.

Season and room temperatureEspecially outdoors

Warm air thins the cloud faster. An outdoor first dance in July needs a different approach from an indoor one in February, and a patio with any breeze across it is a different job again. Telling us the venue and the date is usually enough for us to tell you what to expect.

Fog and light are one decision

A cloud nobody lights is a cloud nobody sees.

This is the part couples most often do not know until they have seen it go wrong somewhere else. Fog is only visible because something is illuminating it. Lit from a low angle it reads as a cloud with depth and edges; lit from directly overhead, or not lit at all, the same cloud barely registers on a phone camera and disappears entirely in a photograph.

So the fog and the lighting get planned together rather than bolted on at the end. A follow spot on the two of you and a warm wash raking across the floor is what turns a machine running in the corner into the picture that was in your head when you asked for it. A custom monogram projected onto the floor picks up a soft halo off the cloud that it never has on bare hardwood.

Everything we bring is delivered, set up and run by our own on-site technician. There is no drop-off and nothing for anyone in your wedding party to operate on the night.

Safety and comfort

What people want to know before they say yes.

Fog effects are safe when they are run properly, and most of the worry couples arrive with is about things that get handled in the setup rather than on the night.

Density is set for the room

The cloud is set so it never obstructs visibility across the floor. That matters most for older guests and for children, who should be able to see where the edge of the dance floor is at all times.

Ventilation and the room

Indoor use depends on the room having sensible airflow, which the vast majority of reception spaces do. It is one of the first things we check when we look at your venue.

Detection systems

A low dry ice cloud stays near the floor rather than rising to ceiling-mounted detectors, which is part of why it is the indoor-friendly option. Venue-specific protocols still get confirmed in advance rather than assumed.

The floor stays clean

The cloud warms, thins and lifts on its own, so the floor is dry and safe underfoot when the song ends and your reception carries straight on.

Somebody is watching it

A technician stays with the effect and adjusts it live, because rooms get warmer as they fill and entrances run late, and both of those change what the cloud does.

Insured, and used to venues

Dallas Event Audio is fully insured and has been working DFW venues since 2001. Certificates go straight to your venue when they need one.

Why it is run, not rented

There is more to this than owning a machine.

The density has to suit the room you are in, which means reading the ceiling height, the temperature, the airflow and the floor before the first cue rather than after it. It has to be timed to the music, not to a guess. It has to clear at the right pace so the photographer gets the shot and the floor is workable a minute later.

And somebody has to be standing there, able to change it in real time, when the room turns out warmer than expected or the entrance runs three minutes late, which at a wedding, it very often does. That is the difference an on-site technician makes, and it is why the effect goes out as something we run rather than something anyone hands over.

Common questions

Wedding fog effects, answered.

Can you use a fog machine indoors?
Yes, and indoors is where the low-lying dry ice effect works best. The cloud stays near the floor instead of rising through the room, so it does not fill the space or drift into the areas where people are eating and talking. What matters is that the venue has reasonable airflow and that any venue-specific protocol is confirmed in advance rather than discovered at load-in, which is a conversation we are happy to have directly with your venue.
Do fog machines set off smoke alarms?
A properly run low-lying dry ice effect is designed to stay at ankle height, well below ceiling-mounted detectors, which is a large part of why it is the indoor-friendly choice. Detection systems do vary between buildings, though, so we confirm the specifics with your venue before the date rather than assuming. Bringing it up early gives us time to check it with them.
How long does the fog last during a first dance?
A low-lying dry ice effect is set to hold for the length of a song, roughly three to four minutes, and then thin out on its own as it warms toward room temperature. It is cued rather than left running, so the cloud is at full depth the moment you step onto the floor and gone shortly after you step off it.
Will fog ruin our wedding photos?
The opposite, when it is set correctly. The whole point of keeping the cloud at ankle height is that your faces stay clear above it while the floor underneath you disappears, which is why the effect photographs so well. The failure mode is a cloud that is too high or lit from the wrong angle, and both of those are setup decisions made before anyone presses anything.
Can we have fog for the grand entrance and the first dance?
Yes, and a lot of couples do. They are two separate cues on the same setup night: a heavier, shorter burst for the doors opening, and a sustained low layer for the song. Tell us both are on the timeline when you book so the room is prepared for each, rather than adding one on the night.
Does the fog leave the floor slippery?
No. A dry ice effect is condensed water vapor carried on carbon dioxide gas, and it warms, thins and lifts rather than settling. There is no residue on the floor, nothing to mop and no gap in your reception while somebody deals with it.

The whole Metroplex, roughly a forty-mile radius from the mid-cities, covering Dallas, Fort Worth, Arlington, Plano, Frisco, Irving, Southlake, McKinney, Grapevine, Denton and everywhere between them. If your venue sits further out than that, ask anyway and we will tell you honestly whether it works.
