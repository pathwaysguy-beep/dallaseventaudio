White and amber are what make pastels possible

Red, green and blue on their own give you saturated color. White lifts a saturated color toward a pastel, which is how blush, ice blue and champagne happen at all. Amber warms skin tones and keeps a room from looking like a computer monitor.

The UV fader looks broken until you aim it at white

UV puts out almost no visible light of its own. Point it at white drape, linens or shirts carrying optical brightener and the fabric starts glowing. Set the surface to white drape and push UV up by itself to see it.

The wall decides half of the color

The same fader positions land differently on white drape, on a gray ballroom wall and on brick, because a wall reflects only what it already contains. Deep blue on brick nearly disappears. That is why we walk your venue before we promise a color, and why we set color on site rather than from a swatch emailed ahead of time.

A real room adds light of its own

This is a simulation, built for the fun of it. House lights, candles, window light at golden hour and whatever your photographer is bouncing off the ceiling all move the answer, which is what the walkthrough on the day of your event is for.

Wedding Uplighting

Uplighting changes the entire room at once.

Every venue looks like itself under the house lights. Beige walls stay beige, the ceiling stays wherever it is, and the photos come back looking like a conference room with flowers in it. Uplighting is what turns that room into your room: a ring of lights around the perimeter, aimed up the walls and columns, washing the whole space in a color you chose.

It is the first thing we recommend on most wedding lighting lists, because it works on the whole space rather than one corner of it, and every photograph taken that night is taken in the light you chose. It also does the thing nobody thinks to ask for: it hides what you do not want seen, such as a dated carpet, a service door, a wall of banquet chairs stacked in the corner.

We place them the afternoon of your event, walk the room with the color up, and adjust until the wash is even. Then we come back at the end of the night and take them out. You never touch them.

Uplighting In Detail

Where the uplights go.

“Uplighting” sounds like one thing you either have or do not have. In practice it is six or seven separate decisions about surfaces, and the difference between a room that looks designed and a room that looks lit is which of them somebody thought about.

The Base Layer

Perimeter wall wash

Fixtures spaced evenly around the outside of the room, aimed straight up the walls. This is what people mean when they say uplighting, and it is the layer everything else sits on top of. Spacing is the whole game. Too far apart and you get scallops of light with dark gaps between them, which reads as cheap even to people who could not tell you why.

Watch for Long unbroken walls, which need more fixtures than their square footage suggests.
Structure

Columns, arches & architectural detail

Columns, pilasters, archways, alcoves, beams and stone detail are the features that a venue was designed around, and house lighting almost always flattens them. A fixture at the base of a column throws light and shadow up its full height. It is the same trick used on buildings at night, and it gives a flat banquet room vertical depth it does not otherwise have.

Watch for Historic venues, hotel ballrooms with pilasters, barns with exposed posts and trusses.
The Photo Wall

Drape, backdrop & head table

The wall behind the head table, the sweetheart table or the stage is in a disproportionate number of your photographs, so it gets treated separately from the rest of the perimeter. Washing drape from below gives fabric its folds back; a flat wall behind a couple reads better in a slightly different tone from the rest of the room, so they separate from it rather than disappearing into it.

Watch for Head tables, sweetheart tables, stages, step-and-repeat and sponsor walls.
Where People Stand

Bars, lounges & cocktail spaces

Cocktail hour and the bar area are usually lit by whatever the venue happens to have, which is often nothing. Uplighting a bar front, a lounge grouping or a cocktail area carries your color into the parts of the night that happen before and around dinner, so the evening looks like one event rather than a lit room and some corridors.

Watch for Separate cocktail rooms, foyers, pre-function space, outdoor patios.
Outside

Outdoor & facade uplighting

Trees, hedges, a building facade, a tent’s poles and peaks, the entrance guests walk through. Outdoor uplighting is what stops an evening event from ending at the doorway, and it is the first thing arriving guests see. It is also a power, weather and fixture-rating conversation before it is a design one, which is where battery fixtures earn their keep.

Watch for Tented receptions, ranch and estate venues, entrances, outdoor ceremonies.
Overhead

Ceilings & structure

In rooms with something worth showing overhead (a coffered ceiling, exposed trusses, a tent’s interior peak) light aimed up into it lifts the whole space and makes a low room feel taller. In rooms with nothing worth showing, we leave it alone, because lighting an ugly ceiling only makes it more visible.

Watch for Tents, barns, atriums, historic halls, anywhere with height going unused.
How An Uplighting Plan Is Built

The six things we work out before a single fixture is loaded.

This is the part that does not show up in a rental listing, and it is most of what you are paying someone for.

Spacing and fixture countMeasured, not guessed

We work from the room’s actual wall lengths and lay fixtures out so their beams overlap slightly before they reach the top of the wall. That overlap is what produces an unbroken wash instead of a row of separate glowing patches. The math decides it, and getting it wrong is a common failure in an under-specified uplighting package.

Wall height and beam angleHow far the light travels

A ten-foot banquet room wall and a twenty-four-foot barn wall need different beam angles to fill properly. Get it wrong in one direction and the color stops a third of the way up; get it wrong in the other and it spills across the ceiling. We ask about ceiling height early because it changes the fixture choice as well as the count.

Battery runtime versus your timelineFrom doors to last dance

Wireless fixtures are freeing precisely because nothing is plugged in, which means runtime has to cover your whole event with margin, including the ceremony, the photos before doors, and the reception running long, which it usually does. We plan to the real length of your night rather than the four hours on the contract.

Fixed color or programmed changesOne look or several

The simplest version is one color set at load-in that holds all night. The better version, in most rooms, changes: warm and low through dinner, then something with more energy when the floor opens. Our technician programs that sequence into presets before doors open, and your DJ or planner recalls each one with a single button press, so the room follows the night. Color changes called by hand during the event are available when a technician is already on site for your booking, which is usually the case when you have audio, a follow spot or a fuller production package with us.

What the house lights are doingThe hidden variable

Uplighting competes with whatever the venue has on its own dimmers. A ballroom at full house level will wash out any color you put on the walls. Part of our job is knowing which venues can dim, which cannot, and who to talk to about it before the day, because in a room that cannot dim the design has to change.

How it photographsNot the same as how it looks

Cameras and eyes disagree. Saturated blues and magentas look striking in the room and can make skin tones look wrong in every photo taken in front of them. We plan colors that hold up in both, coordinate with your photographer or videographer when they have preferences, and tell you when the color you want will not be kind to your photographs.

What we usually pair with the uplights.

The Floor

Dance floor & DJ lighting

Moving heads, wash fixtures and effect lighting focused on the dance floor and run to the music, so the energy changes when the set does. Sized to the floor rather than the room. A dance floor lit like a nightclub in a room of eighty people reads as too much, and we would rather tell you that than sell it to you.

Best for Receptions, private parties, quinceañeras, school and college events, club nights.
Your Name, Ten Feet Tall

Monogram & gobo projection

A custom monogram, initials, date, logo or pattern projected onto a wall, a dance floor or the ceiling. We cut the gobo from your artwork, focus it on site and keep it sharp from the back of the room. Corporate events use the same fixture for a logo beside the stage.

Best for Wedding dance floors, sponsor walls, brand activations, award nights.
Precision

Pin spot lighting

Tight, narrow beams aimed at one thing and nothing else: the centerpieces, the cake, the head table, the escort card display. Pin spots are why the flowers you paid a florist for still read as flowers once the room goes dark, instead of disappearing into a dim table.

Best for Centerpieces, cake tables, head tables, sweetheart tables, displays.
Faces, Not Silhouettes

Stage & ceremony wash

Front light on the people who are being watched: the couple at the altar, the band, the keynote speaker, the award recipient. Rooms are almost always lit from above and behind, which is flattering to the ceiling and ruinous to faces on camera. A wash from the front is what makes the video worth keeping.

Best for Ceremonies, live bands, keynotes, awards, anything being filmed or streamed.
The Big Moments

Effects & specialty lighting

Follow spot for a grand entrance or a first dance, low-lying fog for a dancing-on-the-clouds first dance, and full laser shows for events that want the room to go somewhere else entirely. These make a moment rather than a room. They pay off on one or two cues rather than across the whole night.

Best for First dances, grand entrances, reveals, after-parties, club and rave nights.

Venue By Venue

What uplighting does in a Dallas-Fort Worth venue like yours.

The same fixtures do very different things depending on the walls they are pointed at. This is the conversation we would have with you on a site visit.

Hotel ballroomNeutral walls, low ceiling

The classic case. Beige or gray walls, patterned carpet, a ceiling full of house downlights that flatten everything. Uplighting around the perimeter replaces the room’s color entirely, and dropping the house lights once dinner is served is what makes it land. Ballrooms are also where an even wash matters most, because the walls are long, flat and unforgiving of gaps.

Barn & ranch venueWood, beams, height

Wood eats light, so barns need more fixtures than their square footage suggests, and warmer colors read far better than cool ones on stained timber. The win here is the vertical structure: posts, beams and trusses lit from below give the space depth that a string of bistro lights alone never will.

Historic & brickTexture worth showing

Exposed brick, stone and plaster are the one case where the wall is already the feature. Uplighting is aimed at raking across the surface rather than flooding it, so the texture throws its own shadows. Restrained color and more contrast, so the building is revealed rather than repainted.

Country club & private diningWindows and warm wood

Lots of glass, which means the look changes completely between the cocktail hour and full dark. We plan for both, and we plan around the reflections, because an uplight aimed at a window wall at nine o’clock is lighting the parking lot.

Tent & outdoor receptionNo walls to wash

Without walls, the light has to go somewhere else: up the tent poles and into the peaks, along the perimeter drapes, into the trees at the edge of the site. Outdoor lighting is also a power and weather conversation before it is a design conversation, and battery fixtures solve most of it.

Church & chapelFixed lighting, high ceilings

Ceremony spaces usually have house lighting that cannot be changed and a venue that limits what may be attached to anything. The work is front light on the couple and gentle accents that respect the space, and coordinating early, because the venue’s rules decide this one.

Choosing Your Color

Choosing an uplighting color that works in your room.

Clients usually arrive with a swatch from the florist or a screenshot from Pinterest, and the honest answer is that a color on fabric and the same color on a wall are not the same color. Deep jewel tones read rich in a large ballroom and heavy in a small one. Blush and champagne look beautiful in person and can vanish on camera. Sharp blues photograph dramatically but drain the warmth out of skin tones on the dance floor.

So we talk about it. Bring your palette, your venue and your photographer’s preferences, and we will tell you what that combination does in that room, including when the answer is a warmer version of what you asked for.

Colors can also change through the night: one tone for the ceremony, a second for dinner, something with more energy when the dancing starts. That is a programming decision we make once, and it lands on a button.

If you want to see how a color behaves on a given surface before you commit to it, mix one on the virtual wall at the top of this page and switch the surface underneath it.

Planning Your Budget

What drives the size of an uplighting quote.

We will not put a number on a page, because a number on a page is always wrong for somebody. What we can do is tell you honestly what moves it, so you know what you are trading when you plan.

Wall length before guest countThe main driver

Uplighting is priced by how much wall there is rather than how many people are in front of it. A long narrow ballroom for a hundred guests can need more fixtures than a compact square room for two hundred. This is why we ask about the venue before anything else.

How many elements you addUplights, floor, monogram, pins

Uplighting on its own is the entry point. Dance floor lighting, a monogram, pin spots on the centerpieces and a ceremony wash are each separate additions. Most rooms do well with two or three of them, and we will tell you which ones make the biggest difference to yours.

Load-in conditionsAccess, timing, stairs

A venue with a loading dock and a four-hour window is a different job from a third-floor historic room with a service elevator and a ninety-minute turn between events. Tell us early and there are no surprises later.

Whether we are already thereBundling with audio or DJ

If we are handling your sound system, your DJ or your AV, the lighting shares the same trip, the same crew and the same setup. Booking it together is more efficient than adding it to a separate vendor’s invoice.

The honest version

Send us the date, the venue name and roughly how many guests, and you will have a real quote back, with the fixture count and the plan for your room spelled out in it. If uplighting alone is the right call for your room, we will say so rather than talking you up.

Questions

Uplighting rental, answered plainly.

How many uplights does my room need?
It comes from the length of your walls, not your guest count. We space fixtures so the wash overlaps rather than leaving scallops of light with dark gaps between them, and long rooms need more than their square footage suggests. Send us the venue name and we will work it out from their floor plan. We have been in most Dallas-Fort Worth venues already.
Do the uplights need to be plugged in?
No. Our uplights are battery powered and wireless, so they run the whole event on their own and can be placed anywhere in the room, including against a wall with no outlet behind it. That means no extension cords taped across your floor and no cable running through a doorway your guests are using, which is also what most venues prefer.
Can you match a specific color?
Yes. Bring a swatch, a hex value from your brand guidelines or a photo, and we will match it. We will also be honest with you about what that color does in your particular room, because a shade that looks perfect on fabric can read very differently washed across a wall, and we would rather adjust it with you standing in the room than have you see it for the first time at the reception.
Can the colors change during the event?
Yes, and it is one of the better uses of the system. A warmer tone for the ceremony and dinner, then something with more energy when the dancing starts, is a common program. We build those changes into DMX presets at load-in, one button each, so your DJ or planner shifts the room with the night instead of leaving it on one color for six hours. Color changes called by hand are also available when a technician is already on site for your booking, which is usually the case when you have audio, a follow spot or a fuller production package with us.
Do you deliver and set up, or do I pick the lights up?
We deliver, place, aim, focus, program and strike everything ourselves, and our crew comes back after the event to take every fixture out. There is no pickup counter and no self-service option. Setting the color in the actual room with the house lights at the actual level is most of what makes uplighting look designed rather than rented.
What is a monogram or gobo projector?
It is a focused light with a custom-cut pattern in it, projecting your initials, your names and date, a logo or a decorative design onto a wall, a dance floor or a ceiling. We cut the gobo from your artwork and focus it on site. Corporate clients use the same fixture for a company or sponsor logo beside the stage.
What are pin spots for?
Pin spots are narrow beams aimed at one object: a centerpiece, the cake, the head table, a display. Once the house lights come down for dancing, tall floral arrangements and a cake you spent real money on disappear into a dark table. Pin spots keep them visible and photographable all night.
Do you light the dance floor as well?
Yes. Moving heads, wash fixtures and effects focused on the floor and run to the music, sized to the dance floor rather than the room. It is a different job from uplighting: uplighting sets the mood of the whole space for the entire night, dance floor lighting drives energy in one area for part of it. Most receptions want both, and they should be designed together.
Can I book lighting and sound together?
That is how most of our events run. One vendor means one load-in, one crew, one point of contact and lighting cues that line up with the music, because the same team is running both. If you already have a DJ, we are happy to supply lighting only and coordinate with them directly.
What areas of Dallas-Fort Worth do you cover?
The whole Metroplex: roughly a forty-mile radius from the mid-cities, covering Dallas, Fort Worth, Arlington, Plano, Frisco, Irving, Southlake, McKinney, Richardson, Grapevine, Denton and everywhere in between. If your venue is further out, ask anyway and we will tell you honestly whether it works.
