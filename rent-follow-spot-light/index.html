What A Follow Spot Is

What is a follow spot, and where do you rent one?

A follow spot is a high-output, hard-edged spotlight on a yoke, operated by a person who keeps its beam on someone as they move. It is the light that isolates a bride at the top of a staircase, holds a singer through a solo, or picks an award winner out of a table of ten and walks them to the stage. The defining feature is not the fixture. It is that a human being is aiming it in real time.

That is also the answer to the second half of the question. A follow spotlight is not something you collect from a counter and figure out on the night: it needs a position with a clean sightline, a stable platform or truss, power run to it, a beam and frame size set for your room, and someone standing behind it who knows when the cue is coming. We rent the whole thing as one job, fixture, position, rigging, power and operator.

Everything else on this page is really about that one distinction. See the rest of what we do on the lighting services page, or tell us your venue and we will tell you where the spot needs to live in it.

The Operator Is The Rental

An unmanned spotlight is just a bright light pointed at a wall.

The hard part of a follow spot has never been the fixture. It is knowing to pick someone up two seconds before the doors open rather than two seconds after, sizing the iris down as they come closer so the beam stays on their shoulders instead of swallowing the head table, and easing out under the last note instead of snapping to black.

Those are learned reflexes, and they are why every follow spotlight we send out goes with an operator from our own crew. They walk the room with you or your planner, take the cue list off your timeline, and sit through the rehearsal if there is one. If the timeline slips, and it always slips. There is a person on the spot adjusting rather than a fixture running a program that no longer matches the night.

It also means one less thing on your run sheet. Nobody from your side has to be recruited, briefed, or stationed on a platform in the dark for four hours.

What It Is For

The moments a follow spot exists to serve.

A follow spot is not room lighting and it is not on all night. It earns its place on a handful of cues, the ones people came to see, and the ones that end up in the video.

The Doors Open

Grand entrances

A wedding party, a couple, a guest of honour or a CEO walking into a dark room. The spot picks them up at the door and travels with them to the floor, which does two things at once: it tells four hundred people where to look, and it lights the faces that every phone in the room is already pointed at. Without it, the entrance happens somewhere in a dim doorway and half the room misses it.

Best for Weddings, quinceañeras, galas, award dinners, milestone birthdays.
Three Minutes That Matter

First dances & spotlight dances

House lights down, everything else off, one beam on two people in the middle of a floor. It is the single most photographed and filmed three minutes of a wedding, and it is also the moment most likely to be lit by nothing but a DJ booth and an exit sign. A follow spot separates the couple from the room completely and follows them if they move, which choreographed dances always do.

Best for First dances, father-daughter and mother-son dances, choreographed routines.
Recognition

Award presentations

The classic use, and the one corporate clients ask for by name. The spot finds the recipient at their table, holds them while they stand, and walks them to the stage, so the applause has somewhere to go and the camera has someone to find. On a long awards program it is also the fastest way to keep the show moving without a stage manager shouting names.

Best for Awards nights, galas, banquets, sales kickoffs, honours and recognition programs.
Production

Theater, dance & pageants

Productions where the spot is written into the script rather than added to it: musical numbers, competition routines, crownings, solos and curtain calls. These need a real operator on a real cue sheet, sometimes two spots working opposite sides of a stage, with frame and color changes called from the booth. This is the version of the job that is closest to theatrical practice, and we run it that way.

Best for Pageants, recitals, competitions, school and community theater, dance productions.
Stage

Concerts, bands & live music

A wash lights a band; a follow spot lights a person. Vocal solos, a guitarist stepping forward, a front-of-stage moment that the fixed rig cannot reach, the spot is what tells an audience where the song is happening. Paired with stage wash and a designed rig it turns a lit stage into a show.

Best for Live bands, concerts, festivals, tribute nights, showcases and battle-of-the-bands.
The Podium

Keynotes & general sessions

Conference general sessions increasingly put the speaker in front of the lectern rather than behind it, and a fixed front wash cannot follow someone who walks. A spot keeps a presenter properly lit anywhere on the stage, which matters most when the session is being recorded or streamed, a keynote that drifts out of the light is a keynote that cannot be used again.

Best for Conferences, general sessions, town halls, product reveals, streamed and recorded events.
Worship

Church & houses of worship

Christmas and Easter productions, dramas, youth events, choir and worship-team features. Sanctuaries are usually lit for a fixed platform and nothing else, so anything staged outside that platform disappears. A spot from the back of the room covers the aisle, the floor and the balcony steps, and it goes in without touching the building.

Best for Holiday productions, dramas, baptisms, youth nights, guest speakers.
School

Schools, colleges & graduations

Homecoming courts, talent shows, dance team features, honours ceremonies and graduation walks in gymnasiums that were built for basketball and lit accordingly. The spot is usually the only lighting decision that changes the whole feel of the event, which is why it is often the one thing a school books.

Best for Homecoming, prom, talent shows, graduations, honours assemblies, college productions.
Nightlife

Club nights & special reveals

Bottle service arrivals, artist introductions, a car or product coming out from behind drape, a countdown moment. A hard beam cutting through haze is a different tool from wash and effect lighting, and it is what makes a reveal read as an event rather than a curtain being pulled.

Best for Club and rave nights, artist intros, product reveals, launches, New Year countdowns.

Spotlight Rental, Sorted Out

“Spotlight rental” means four different things. Here is which one you want.

People arrive on this page asking for a spotlight and mean any one of these. They are genuinely different fixtures doing genuinely different jobs, and asking for the wrong one is the most common way an event ends up with lighting that does not do what was pictured.

Follow spotMoves with a person

A large, high-output spotlight on a yoke with an operator behind it, an iris to size the beam and a frame to shape it. It is the only one of these that tracks somebody as they move. If the picture in your head involves a beam following a person across a floor, this is the one, and it is what the rest of this page is about.

Pin spotAimed at an object, and it stays there

A small fixture throwing a tight beam at one fixed thing, a centerpiece, the cake, the head table, a display case. It is set at load-in and never moves again, and there is no operator. People often say “spotlight the cake” when what they want is a pin spot, which is a much smaller undertaking. We cover pin spots as part of a lighting design.

Stage or ceremony washCovers an area, not a person

Front light across a defined space so faces read properly for the room, for photography and on camera. A wash is what stops a stage from being a silhouette; it is not dramatic and it is not supposed to be. Many events that ask for a follow spot actually need a wash first and a spot second, and we will say so.

Moving head spotAutomated, programmed, not tracking a person

A motorised intelligent fixture that pans, tilts and changes color on a programmed cue. It can hit a mark beautifully and it can sweep a room, but it is following a timeline rather than a human being, so it cannot adapt when a bride stops to hug her grandmother halfway down the aisle. Great for looks and reveals, not a substitute for an operated spot.

Not sure which one your moment needs?

Describe what you are picturing, who is moving, where they are going and how dark the room is, and we will tell you which fixture actually does it. Sometimes the honest answer is a pin spot and a wash rather than a follow spot, and that is a smaller conversation than the one you thought you were having. Start at our quote form.

Venue By Venue

Where the spot goes in a room like yours.

Position is decided before anything else, because it determines the angle on faces, whether the beam clears the crowd, and what it lands on when it misses. This is the conversation we would have on a site visit.

Hotel ballroomLow ceiling, no rigging points

The most common case and the most constrained one. Ceilings are often too low for a steep angle, so the spot goes on a platform at the back of the room and works at a flatter throw. That is workable, but it means the beam has to clear standing guests and a stack of centerpieces, which is exactly the calculation nobody makes until the night. We make it in advance.

Theater & auditoriumPurpose-built positions

The easiest venues to work in, because someone already solved this: a booth, a catwalk or a balcony rail with power and sightlines. Our job here is mostly matching the house rig, working with the resident technician, and running cues to a stage manager’s calls rather than a wedding timeline.

Church & sanctuaryFixed lighting, protected surfaces

Sanctuaries usually light one platform and nothing else, and most limit what may be attached to the building. We work from a freestanding position at the back or from an existing balcony, cover the aisle and the floor as well as the platform, and leave the structure untouched. Coordinating with the facilities team early settles most of it.

Gymnasium & multipurpose roomHeight, but nothing to hang from

Plenty of ceiling, no useful rigging, and overhead lighting that is either full-on or off. Ground-supported positions and a proper platform do the work. The gain is dramatic here precisely because the baseline is so flat, a single operated beam changes a gym more than anything else you could bring into it.

Barn, ranch & historic venueStructure, sightlines and rules

Exposed trusses and lofts sometimes give a genuinely excellent position, and sometimes give an obstructed one. Historic venues add rules about what can be placed where. We look at the actual room before committing to a position, because a spot in the wrong place is worse than no spot at all.

Tent & outdoorPower, weather and no back wall

Outdoors there is rarely power where the spot needs to be and rarely a structure to put it on, so the plan starts with a tower or platform and a power run. Wind, dew and temperature all get factored in. It is very doable. It is just a conversation that has to happen well before the week of.

What’s Included

What a follow spot rental should include, and usually does not.

A trained operator

Someone from our crew runs the spot for the whole event. This is not an add-on and it is not optional. It is the part that makes the rest of it work.

Position and platform

We work out where the spot lives, bring the platform or ground support to put it there, and check the sightline against a room full of standing people, not an empty one.

Power and cabling

Power run to the position, routed and secured so nothing crosses a guest path untaped. We confirm the circuit with the venue rather than finding out at load-in.

A cue list

We take your timeline and turn it into cues, who, where, when, and what happens either side. Then we bring a printed copy to the position.

Rehearsal attendance

Where there is a rehearsal or a soundcheck, our operator is at it. Blocking that only exists in someone’s head is the main reason spots miss.

Comms

The operator is on comms with whoever is calling the show, your planner, DJ, stage manager or our own audio position, so a late change reaches the spot.

Venue paperwork

Certificates of insurance sent to your venue on request, and we work to their load-in windows and rules. We have been in most DFW venues before.

Load-in and strike

We bring it, rig it, focus it, run it and take it away. There is no pickup counter, nothing dropped at a loading dock and nothing left for you to return.

One vendor for lighting and sound

Pairs Well With

What usually goes with a follow spot.

A spot works by contrast, so it is at its best when the rest of the room is designed rather than left to the house lights. These are the things most often booked alongside it.

The Room

Uplighting & color wash

Battery-powered wireless uplights around the perimeter give the room a color and a mood the spot can cut through. Bright house lights are the single biggest thing working against a follow spot, uplighting is what lets you turn them down and still have a room.

Best for Receptions, galas, banquets, anywhere the walls are doing nothing for you.
Faces First

Stage & ceremony wash

Front light across the stage or altar so faces read for the room, the photographer and the camera. A wash and a spot do opposite jobs and belong together: the wash makes people visible, the spot makes one of them the subject.

Best for Ceremonies, keynotes, bands, award stages, anything filmed or streamed.
Atmosphere

Haze & low-lying fog

A beam is invisible until there is something in the air for it to hit. A light haze is what turns a follow spot into a visible shaft of light rather than just a bright circle on the floor, and low-lying fog puts a first dance on a cloud. Both need clearance with the venue, which we handle.

Best for First dances, reveals, concerts, club nights, dramatic entrances.
The Big Look

Lasers & specialty effects

Staffed laser systems with proper safety planning, for events where a spot alone is not the scale of the moment. Different tool, same principle. It is run live by somebody, not left on a timer.

Best for Concerts, festivals, after-parties, club and rave nights, major reveals.
Branding

Monogram & gobo projection

Your initials, a date, a company logo or a sponsor mark cut as a gobo and projected onto a wall, a floor or a drape. It is a still image where the spot is a moving one, and the two together cover most of what a room needs to feel produced.

Best for Wedding dance floors, sponsor walls, brand activations, award nights.
Sound

Audio, microphones & DJ

Most spot cues are also audio cues, an introduction, a song, an announcement. Running both means the light and the sound arrive together instead of a second apart. Every microphone we send out runs our AI-powered De-Feedback system, so a presenter can walk into the spot without anyone bracing for a squeal. There is more on how that works in our De-Feedback explainer.

Best for Awards, ceremonies, keynotes, live bands, anything with a microphone in it.

Questions

Follow spot rental, answered plainly.

What is a follow spot?
A follow spot is a high-output, hard-edged spotlight on a yoke that an operator aims by hand, keeping the beam on a person as they move. It has an iris to size the beam and a frame to shape it, and it is the only lighting fixture that genuinely tracks somebody in real time. That is what separates it from a pin spot, a wash or a programmed moving head.
Does the follow spot come with an operator?
Always, and it is not an optional extra. Every follow spot we send out goes with a trained operator from our own crew who works from your cue list, attends the rehearsal where there is one, and adapts live when the timeline moves. The fixture is the easy part, the operator is what you are actually renting.
Can we pick one up and run it ourselves?
No. We do not drop equipment off, and we do not hand a follow spot to a volunteer. Beyond the practical reasons, the position, the platform, the power run and the focus all have to be right, an operated spot is a skill, and a missed pickup on a grand entrance is not a mistake you can fix afterwards.
Where does the follow spot go in the room?
Usually at the back, elevated on a platform or ground support so the beam clears a room full of standing guests. Theaters and auditoriums often have a purpose-built booth, catwalk or balcony rail we can use instead. We work the position out from your venue in advance, because it decides the angle on faces and whether the sightline survives a full room.
What is the difference between a follow spot and a pin spot?
A follow spot is large, operated and moves with a person. A pin spot is small, fixed at load-in and aimed at one object, a centerpiece, a cake, a head table. People often say “spotlight the cake” when they mean a pin spot, which is a much smaller undertaking. Tell us what you are picturing and we will tell you which one it is.
Do you need to rig anything to our venue?
Usually not. Most of our positions are freestanding, a platform or ground support that touches nothing but the floor, which is what churches, historic venues and hotels generally require. Where a venue has its own catwalk or balcony position, we will use it. We confirm all of this with your venue before the day.
Do we need one spot or two?
One covers most weddings, awards nights and corporate sessions. Two are worth it when a stage is wide enough that a single side angle would leave someone half-lit, when two people need to be picked up independently, or when a production calls for opposite-side coverage, pageants, dance competitions and theatrical shows most often. We will tell you which yours is.
How do you know when the cues are?
We build a cue list off your timeline or run sheet and agree who is calling the show, whether that is your planner, your DJ, a stage manager or our own audio position. Our operator is on comms with them, has a printed cue sheet at the position, and attends the rehearsal or soundcheck where there is one. Timelines always move, and a person on the spot can move with them.
Can we add a follow spot to lighting or sound we have already booked?
Yes, and plenty of clients do. If you already have a DJ, a band or in-house AV, we supply the spot and the operator and coordinate directly with whoever is calling the show. If we are already handling your audio or lighting design, it shares a load-in, a crew and one cue sheet, which is the version where the light and the music land together.
What areas of Dallas-Fort Worth do you cover?
The whole Metroplex, roughly a forty-mile radius from the mid-cities, covering Dallas, Fort Worth, Arlington, Plano, Frisco, Irving, Southlake, McKinney, Richardson, Grapevine, Denton and everywhere in between. If your venue is further out, ask anyway and we will tell you honestly whether it works.
